Ecopoetics

Course at the intersection of ecology and literary imagination — reading nature writing, environmental poetry, and field literature, then producing original creative work responding to ecological observation. Projects included a published Quarto poetry book and an interactive naturalist field guide built as a web application.

Course Overview

Ecopoetics explores the relationship between ecological science and poetic imagination — how literary language can make environmental observation legible in ways that data alone cannot. The course engaged primary texts from writers including Annie Dillard, Aldo Leopold, Gary Snyder, and Robin Wall Kimmerer, alongside theoretical readings on place, embodiment, and more-than-human worlds.

As a geospatial data scientist, the course pushed me to work in a radically different register: close observation, attention to the particular, and form as a mode of knowing. The two creative projects below reflect that tension — between scientific and poetic ways of engaging with the natural world.

Project 1 — My Week of Potatoes

A week-long observational sequence: I committed to a single organism — the common potato (Solanum tuberosum) — and wrote about it every day for a week. What began as a constraint exercise became something else: a sustained meditation on the domesticated plant, colonial agricultural history, and the strangeness of an organism so familiar it has become invisible.

The resulting poems range from lyric description to procedural form, from the vocabulary of tuber botany to the grocery aisle. The full sequence was compiled and typeset as a Quarto PDF book — formatted as a proper chapbook with frontmatter, section breaks, and consistent typographic design.

My Week of Potatoes — Chapbook

Seven poems in sequence, one per day. Typeset in Quarto with custom PDF theming. The project sits at the boundary of field note and lyric, documentation and invention.

Project 2 — Bloodworm Field Guide

The second project began with a question: what would a naturalist field guide look like if it were built for the web rather than the page?

The subject: bloodworm larvae (Glycera spp.) — bright red, venomous-jawed polychaete worms found in marine sediments, used as fishing bait, and essentially ignored by mainstream natural history illustration despite their remarkable biology.

The field guide is built as an interactive HTML site styled after the visual language of a Pokédex — because the Pokédex, as a genre, is a field guide, and the playful frame opens the bloodworm to the kind of attention it deserves and rarely receives. Each "entry" combines a descriptive profile, behavioral notes, ecological relationships, and a stylized illustration panel.

Bloodworm Pokédex

Interactive HTML field guide in Pokédex format. Built in vanilla HTML/CSS/JS — a deliberate formal choice: the web as naturalist notebook. Each card presents Glycera life stages, feeding behavior, habitat associations, and taxonomic notes in a visual format that makes the organism memorable.
