Spatial Cognition

A course on how people build and use internal representations of space — perception, navigation, and action. Final paper: a literature review and proposed experiment on spatial action without vision.

Final Paper — "Throwing Blind"

Throwing Blind: How Experience and Proprioception Support Spatial Action Without Vision asks how people maintain internal spatial representations and perform goal-directed actions — like throwing — when vision is impaired or unavailable. The question started from a personal observation: with 10/120 uncorrected visual acuity, I found I could still throw accurately in an inner-tube water polo game without glasses, relying on years of ingrained movement patterns and an internal sense of the pool's layout rather than clear visual detail.

The paper reviews literature spanning cognitive science, perceptual psychology, and motor learning to address three questions: how far proprioception and motor memory can compensate for missing visual input in tasks like throwing or navigation; how prior experience with a space affects spatial confidence and accuracy as visual conditions degrade; and what that variation reveals about how internal spatial maps are built, maintained, and used.

Key Argument

Vision is foundational to spatial cognition but not exclusive — it initiates and sharpens spatial representations, especially for large-scale layout comprehension, but once formed, those models can be maintained and used to guide precise action through proprioception, motor memory, and embodied experience alone. Confidence in an internal spatial model varies with familiarity and feedback history, but isn't always tightly linked to accuracy: people often act effectively even while feeling uncertain.

Proposed Experiment

The paper closes with a proposed within-subjects study: participants throw a soft object at a target under four progressively degraded visual conditions — normal vision, simulated blurry vision, blindfolded with prior visual exposure to the space, and blindfolded with no visual exposure at all — while also varying task familiarization (with or without prior practice throws). Accuracy and self-rated confidence are compared across conditions to test whether proprioceptive models can support action as vision degrades, and whether confidence tracks actual performance or diverges from it.
