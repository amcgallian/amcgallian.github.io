Environmental Data Science · Ecological Analysis

Revetment Biodiversity Study

Multi-site ecological survey of macroinvertebrate communities across five Chicago-area revetment habitats — combining alpha diversity, Shannon entropy, Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, NMDS ordination, and hierarchical clustering to characterize community structure and site similarity.

Sites 5 revetment locations Taxa ~10 macroinvertebrate groups Course Environmental Data Science

Overview

Revetments — engineered riparian structures of rock, concrete, or riprap — line much of Chicago's lakefront and river edges. While they serve flood control and erosion functions, their ecological value varies dramatically by substrate type, structural complexity, and surrounding land use.

This project surveys macroinvertebrate communities across five revetment sites in the Chicago area, applying a full suite of community ecology metrics to quantify how biodiversity and species composition differ between sites and what structural features drive those differences.

Study Design

Site Selection

Five revetment sites were selected to span a gradient of structural and habitat complexity — from simple concrete seawalls to complex riprap edges with intersticial crevice habitat. Sites varied in substrate type, riparian vegetation cover, and degree of urban encroachment.

Field Sampling

Macroinvertebrate communities were sampled using standardized protocols at each site. Organisms were identified to the lowest practical taxonomic level, and counts were recorded per sample unit. Approximately 10 macroinvertebrate groups were consistently represented across all five sites.

Analysis Methods

Alpha Diversity & Shannon Entropy

Alpha diversity (species richness per site) and Shannon-Wiener diversity index (H') were calculated for each site. Shannon entropy captures both richness and evenness, giving a fuller picture of community structure than raw species counts alone. Sites with complex riprap habitat consistently showed higher H' values.

Bray-Curtis Dissimilarity

Pairwise Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was computed across all site pairs to quantify compositional distance. Bray-Curtis accounts for species abundances (not just presence/absence), making it appropriate for count data from replicated surveys. The resulting dissimilarity matrix was used as input for both ordination and clustering.

NMDS Ordination

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was applied to the Bray-Curtis matrix to project community composition into two dimensions for visualization. Sites that cluster tightly in NMDS space share similar species assemblages. Stress values were checked to confirm ordination quality.

Hierarchical Clustering

Ward's minimum variance hierarchical clustering was applied to the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix, producing a dendrogram of site similarity. The resulting groupings were interpreted in relation to habitat structure and substrate type.

Radar Plot Visualization

Per-taxon relative abundance profiles for each site were visualized as radar (spider) plots — allowing direct visual comparison of community "shape" across sites. Sites dominated by pollution-tolerant taxa (e.g., chironomids) versus sensitive taxa (e.g., EPT groups) are immediately distinguishable in this format.

Key Findings

NMDS and clustering identified two primary site groupings: structurally complex riprap sites with higher biodiversity and more EPT-group representatives, and simpler concrete/seawall sites with lower diversity dominated by pollution-tolerant chironomid midges. Shannon diversity correlated positively with crevice habitat availability — consistent with the intermediate disturbance hypothesis and with habitat complexity driving local invertebrate diversity at engineered shorelines.

Ecological Significance

The results suggest that the choice of revetment design — not just its presence — has measurable ecological consequences. Riprap structures supporting interstitial habitat host substantially more diverse invertebrate communities than bare concrete surfaces, with implications for restoration and green infrastructure design.
