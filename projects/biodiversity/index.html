Revetments of LaRabida and Promontory Point

A field survey of two Jackson Park lakefront sites — combining DNA barcoding, morphological identification, and an ArcGIS field survey to ask whether revetment design measurably shapes biodiversity, and whether the best ecosystem is also the best erosion control.

Sites LaRabida (5 sections) + Promontory Point Team 5-person, led by Andrew Course Environmental Data Science, Spring 2025

Overview

Revetments — engineered structures of rock, concrete, or rubble that armor a shoreline against erosion — line much of Chicago's lakefront. This project asks whether the choice of revetment design has a measurable effect on the biodiversity it supports, using two Jackson Park sites: the LaRabida Children's Hospital shoreline, divided into five structurally distinct sections (Revetments 1–5), and Promontory Point, used as a qualitative comparison site.

I led a 5-person team (with Clara Deimling, Sanju Patel, Christian Herrera, and Nic Restivo) through six weeks of field sampling, DNA barcoding in the lab, and the spatial/statistical analysis that turned raw specimens into a site-by-site biodiversity comparison.

Field & Lab Methods

Specimen Collection

Field work ran twice a week for four weeks at LaRabida and Promontory Point. Every uniquely distinguishable specimen was logged with its site location, a morphological description, a photograph, and an uncontaminated sample collected in a vial or bag.

DNA Barcoding

Species identification combined morphology with DNA barcoding: DNA was extracted with Qiagen DNeasy kits, PCR-amplified targeting the COI region for animals and rbCL for plants, and sequenced at the University of Chicago core facility. Sequences were matched against known references via DNA Subway's BLAST feature, cross-checked against dichotomous keys, the Field Museum's Chicago moss guide, and iNaturalist, and each identification was tagged as native or exotic using USDA PLANTS and Kew Plants of the World data.

ArcGIS Field Survey

A final survey week used an ArcGIS Survey123 form to log every organism found along transects at 0, 10, and 30 feet from the shoreline across Revetments 1–5, recording location, broad organism type, and an image at each point — the dataset the area-normalized diversity metrics below are built from.

Analysis

For each revetment section, I computed alpha diversity (raw and per-m² richness), Shannon diversity index, evenness, and pairwise Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, then used NMDS ordination to visualize how community composition clustered across sites. Phylogenetic trees were built per revetment from the barcoded sequences to check whether revetment type filtered species by evolutionary lineage. Species without a direct identification were placed into a "constructed ecosystem" food web per site, built from literature on predation relationships and comparable Chicago-area ecosystems, to make qualitative comparisons possible even where genetic data was incomplete.

Key Findings

Forbs were the most abundant category overall, followed by mosses and invertebrates. Revetment 2 was the most distinct site — highest Bray-Curtis dissimilarity from the others, the widest phylogenetic spread (five plant orders), and the densest invertebrate and lichen presence, likely driven by its jagged, gap-rich rubble structure. Revetment 5 had the highest richness density but the lowest evenness — a single-category-dominated community. Revetment 3 was the most balanced (highest Shannon index). Moss species were overwhelmingly native, while forbs and invertebrates were dominated by exotic and invasive species — a pattern with real implications for which revetment types are worth prioritizing for native biodiversity.

Biodiversity vs. Erosion Control

Cross-referencing the biodiversity results against field observations of erosion performance, Revetments 2 and 3 — both rubble-based, with irregular blocks and open spacing — were also the most effective at dissipating wave energy. Revetment 3 stood out as likely the most cost-effective of the five (its rubble reads as reused, discarded concrete) while also posting the highest Shannon diversity value, making it the strongest overall candidate among the five when biodiversity, erosion control, and cost are weighed together.
