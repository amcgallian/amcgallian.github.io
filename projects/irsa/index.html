Wildfire Risk Assessment from Multi-Source Remote Sensing

Multi-layer wildfire risk model for a Contra Costa County, California, study area, integrating LIDAR-derived canopy structure, fuel type classification, PRISM precipitation surfaces, and topographic slope — with final risk maps delivered as a conference-style poster.

Course Intro to Remote Sensing Study Area Contra Costa County, CA Output Wildfire risk surface + poster

Overview

This project builds a composite wildfire risk model for a Contra Costa County, California using four independent remote sensing data sources. Rather than relying on a single sensor, the analysis combines structural, spectral, climatic, and topographic inputs — each contributing a distinct dimension of risk — into a weighted multi-criteria risk surface.

The final outputs were compiled into a conference-style research poster presenting the methodology, intermediate raster layers, and the classified risk map.

Data Sources

LIDAR — Canopy Height & Structure

Airborne LIDAR point cloud data was processed to derive canopy height models (CHM) and canopy cover metrics. Dense, tall canopies represent elevated fuel loads and vertical fuel continuity — both key predictors of crown fire potential. LIDAR-derived height bands were classified into fuel structure risk tiers.

Fuel Type Classification

Multispectral imagery was classified using the National Forest Fuel Model system (Anderson 13 / Scott & Burgan 40) to assign fuel type categories across the landscape. High-risk fuel models (chaparral, closed timber litter, slash) were weighted more heavily in the composite risk calculation.

PRISM Climate — Precipitation

PRISM gridded 30-year normal annual precipitation surfaces were used as a proxy for vegetation moisture and drought susceptibility. Drier sub-regions within the study area received higher risk weights, capturing the role of moisture deficit in fire behavior.

Topography — Slope

A 10-meter DEM was used to derive slope in degrees. Steep terrain accelerates fire spread upslope and complicates suppression access. Slope was reclassified into five risk tiers (0–10°, 10–20°, 20–30°, 30–40°, >40°) and entered as the fourth risk component.

Analysis & Methods

All four inputs were resampled to a common 30-meter grid and reclassified to a 1–5 risk scale. A weighted linear combination was applied:

  • Fuel type: 35% weight (highest driver of fire behavior)
  • Canopy height/structure: 30%
  • Slope: 20%
  • Precipitation deficit: 15%

The composite risk surface was classified into five output categories (Very Low to Very High) and symbolized using a sequential color ramp. High-risk zones cluster in steep, south-facing slopes with closed-canopy timber fuel models and low precipitation normals.
