Clearing the Air: Aerosol Optical Depth Distribution in the USA

22 years of MODIS Aqua aerosol retrievals (2003–2025) over the contiguous US. A flat national trend conceals a real story underneath — Southeast air quality improving in step with EPA coal plant retirements, offset nationally by worsening western wildfire smoke.

Study area Contiguous USA Data MODIS Aqua, 2003–2025 Course Environmental Data Science II
Read the Full Write-up →

Overview

Aerosol optical depth (AOD) is the column-integrated measure of how much sunlight is scattered or absorbed by aerosol particles in the atmosphere — a satellite-observable proxy for particulate air pollution, linked to PM2.5 concentrations and respiratory health outcomes. This project uses 22 years of MODIS Aqua AOD retrievals (Deep Blue / Dark Target merged product, 550 nm) over the contiguous US to ask: has national air quality trended over two decades, and if so, why?

The national annual mean shows no significant trend (p = 0.449). But that flat average hides a real divergence: the distribution has grown significantly more right-skewed over time (p = 0.007), meaning extreme pollution episodes are becoming more common even as the average holds steady. Tracing that divergence spatially led to the EPA's Mercury and Air Toxics Standards (MATS, finalized 2012) — a regulation that forced a wave of coal plant retirements concentrated in the Southeast — as the primary structural driver, partially offset nationally by intensifying western wildfire smoke.

Key Finding

Coal belt cities (states drawing primarily on Appalachian/Illinois Basin coal) went from higher median AOD than other US cities pre-MATS (0.162 vs. 0.128) to lower post-MATS (0.139 vs. 0.150) — a complete reversal (t = −5.80, p < 0.001), independent of population growth. Global Moran's I — a measure of spatial clustering — declined in three phases tracking the MATS compliance window (2003–2014 stable, 2014–2021 declining, 2021–present partial recovery from wildfire-driven western clustering).

Method

Three analytical tools anchor the study: OLS time series regression on national and city-level AOD series, Holt-Winters exponential smoothing to separate structural trend from seasonal signal, and Global/Local Moran's I to quantify spatial clustering over time. ARIMA modeling was also attempted but proved uninformative — AOD's extremes are driven by structural events (policy, wildfire) rather than smooth autoregressive dynamics, which is itself part of the finding.

City-level AOD was extracted via 10 km buffers around the top 100 US cities and compared across three regulatory periods (pre-MATS 2003–2011, during 2012–2016, post 2017–2024), with cities classified as coal belt or not using EIA 2008 electric power sector consumption data. Population growth was tested and ruled out as a confound — within coal belt cities it explains nothing about AOD trend direction (p = 0.643).

Mean AOD difference (2016–2023 vs. 2003–2011) — blue = improvement, red = worsening. Southeast reductions align with coal plant retirements; Western worsening tracks post-2017 wildfire intensification.
