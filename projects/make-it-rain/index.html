MS Capstone · Spring 2026 · with Joshua Frank

No Evidence of Cloud Seeding Signatures in the NEXRAD Radar Record

An unsupervised anomaly-detection framework — a convolutional autoencoder feeding a causal transformer — testing whether operational cloud seeding leaves a detectable signature in the NEXRAD radar archive, across two contrasting case studies.

Mentor Jim Franke Case studies SNOWIE (Idaho) · Rainmaker (West Texas) Compute UChicago Midway3 cluster

Abstract

Cloud seeding has been practiced operationally for decades, yet evidence for its efficacy remains difficult to isolate due to strong natural variability and non-random operational targeting. This project investigates whether cloud seeding operations leave detectable signatures in the NEXRAD radar record using an unsupervised anomaly detection framework. Composite NEXRAD reflectivity mosaics were processed into 256×256 pixel tiled radar scenes and encoded using a convolutional autoencoder. Temporal evolution in latent space was then modeled using a causal transformer trained on non-event radar sequences.

The framework was evaluated on two contrasting case studies: the SNOWIE winter orographic cloud seeding experiment in Idaho and the Trans-Pecos Weather Modification Association (TPWMA) operational convective seeding program in West Texas. The k-NN anomaly scorer achieved AUROCs of 0.841 (SNOWIE) and 0.742 (Rainmaker); the transformer reached AUROC 0.9605 on Rainmaker test sequences. Despite this separation, results do not provide clean evidence of cloud-seeding-specific signatures in the NEXRAD record.

Study domains — SNOWIE (Idaho) and Rainmaker/TPWMA (West Texas)

Datasets

SNOWIE (Seeded and Natural Orographic Wintertime Clouds: The Idaho Experiment) was a winter field campaign in the Payette River Basin, Idaho, 2016–2017 — one of the first cloud seeding experiments in the US to employ a randomized treatment design alongside close-range Doppler-on-Wheels radar, directly documenting silver iodide seeding plume structures.

Rainmaker (TPWMA) is an operational warm-season convective cloud seeding program run by the Trans-Pecos Weather Modification Association in West Texas, using aircraft-mounted glaciogenic and hygroscopic flares under contract with agricultural and ranching interests. Unlike SNOWIE, TPWMA seeding decisions are made in real time with no randomized controls — operations records (flight timing, flare counts, target counties) were compiled from publicly available Trans-Pecos operations reports.

Method

Data Processing

Radar mosaics were spatially tiled into 256×256 pixel patches (25% overlap), with tiles below a minimum reflectivity threshold discarded as clear-sky frames. Training and test sets were separated temporally to prevent leakage between background and seeding-event periods — roughly 908K training / 1.7K test tiles for Rainmaker across 46 seeding days, and 3.86M training / 7K test tiles for SNOWIE across 24 intensive observation periods.

Convolutional Autoencoder

A convolutional autoencoder learned compact 256-dimensional latent representations of ordinary radar structure — four convolutional blocks (1→16→32→64→128 channels) into a three-layer MLP, trained on background (non-seeding) frames only, so the model never saw labeled examples of seeding effects.

k-NN Anomaly Scoring

Per-tile anomaly scores were computed via k-nearest-neighbor cosine distance in the normalized latent space, using a FAISS index over training-set latent vectors. Tiles whose radar structure differs from the training distribution — including seeding-event frames — receive higher anomaly scores.

Causal Transformer

To model temporal radar evolution, latent vectors were organized into per-tile-position time series and used to train a causal transformer — a decoder-only architecture in the style of Vaswani et al. (2017), 4 layers, 4 attention heads, 256-dimensional embeddings. The transformer received 12 consecutive latent vectors (one hour of 5-minute scans) as context and predicted the 13th; anomaly score at inference is the cosine distance between predicted and observed latent vectors.

Results

Within SNOWIE, the model could separate seeding-period tiles from ordinary background radar (AUROC 0.841) but could not discriminate seeded from unseeded intensive observation periods — unseeded control missions in fact scored higher on average, indicating the model was responding to unusual storm morphology rather than seeding specifically. Within Rainmaker, four structurally anomalous seeding days were flagged (exceeding background p99.9 by 5–6×), and the causal transformer reached AUROC 0.9605 on one-hour latent sequences — a substantially stronger separation than k-NN scoring alone, though still confounded by TPWMA's practice of seeding storms that were already intensifying.

Discussion & Conclusion

The results demonstrate both the utility and the limitations of unsupervised radar anomaly detection for evaluating cloud seeding operations. Two constraints dominate: a detectability limit — SNOWIE's seeded plumes were shallow, low-reflectivity ice-crystal signatures likely below NEXRAD's effective sensitivity at operational range — and an attribution limit — Rainmaker's seeding decisions are made in real time based on already-favorable storm conditions, so the same conditions that motivate seeding also explain the anomalous radar evolution that follows.

Overall, the study does not find clean evidence of a distinct NEXRAD-scale cloud seeding signature. The absence of such a signature does not demonstrate the absence of cloud seeding effects — it clarifies the observational and methodological constraints radar-based evaluation operates under, and demonstrates unsupervised ML as a screening tool for identifying meteorologically unusual events within large radar archives.
