Landsat LST Processing Methods

In-depth methods for retrieving and processing land surface temperature from Landsat thermal infrared bands — the reference document behind the Landsat side of the Environmental Frontiers pipeline.

1. Thermal Infrared Radiation and Radiometric Calibration

Landsat's Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) on Landsat 8, specifically Bands 10 and 11, measures emitted radiance in the thermal infrared spectrum, centered around 10.9 µm and 12 µm wavelengths. This spectral range is ideal for capturing the thermal emission characteristics of land surfaces, as it falls within an atmospheric window where water vapor and other gases minimally absorb thermal radiation (Gao et al., 2006).

Each thermal band sensor records digital numbers (DNs) that correspond to the radiance emitted from Earth's surface. These DNs are converted to at-sensor radiance using a calibration equation:

L_sensor = (Gain × DN) + Offset

where Gain and Offset are sensor-specific calibration coefficients provided in the Landsat metadata (Chander et al., 2009). This conversion allows retrieval of the radiance in physical units (W/m²/sr/µm).

To approximate the radiance reaching the sensor without atmospheric interference, further corrections are applied based on atmospheric transmittance, path radiance, and downwelling radiance — factors that account for the atmosphere's partial absorption and scattering of thermal radiation. This top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiance calculation ensures the data are comparable across different time frames and conditions.

2. Land Surface Temperature (LST) Calculation

TOA radiance values are converted to brightness temperature using an inversion of Planck's law, which relates the radiance emitted by a blackbody to its temperature:

T_sensor = K2 / ln(K1 / L_sensor + 1)

Here, K1 and K2 are thermal calibration constants specific to each Landsat thermal band (Wang et al., 2016). Brightness temperature provides the apparent temperature at the sensor, yet does not directly represent surface temperature due to the effects of emissivity and atmospheric influence.

Since land surfaces are not perfect blackbodies, the emissivity (ε) of each pixel is factored in to convert brightness temperature into LST. Emissivity varies by surface type (vegetation, soil, water) and can be approximated through indices such as NDVI:

LST = T_sensor / (1 + (λ × T_sensor / ρ) × ln ε)

where λ represents the wavelength of emitted radiance and ρ is a constant based on Planck's law (Kumar & Stigter, 2012). This correction aligns the retrieved temperature with the actual surface temperature by accounting for the varying emissivity of natural surfaces.

3. Atmospheric Correction

Atmospheric conditions can alter thermal radiation by absorbing or scattering energy before it reaches the satellite. Radiative transfer models like MODTRAN or the Single-Channel algorithm estimate the atmospheric contribution based on ancillary data (water vapor content, aerosol optical depth) at the time of acquisition (Kumar et al., 2013). These corrections adjust TOA radiance to approximate the radiance emitted directly from the Earth's surface.

The split-window algorithm, using Bands 10 and 11, leverages the difference in radiance between the two channels to more accurately estimate LST by accounting for atmospheric absorption differentials between them (Gonzalez et al., 2016).

4. Resampling: From 100 m to 30 m Grid Cells

Landsat TIRS captures thermal data at 100 m native resolution, while surface reflectance bands are at 30 m. To integrate thermal data with other spectral bands, thermal bands are resampled to the 30 m grid using bilinear or cubic convolution interpolation:

  • Bilinear interpolation averages the four nearest 100 m pixels to estimate the value of a 30 m pixel, smoothing transitions and preserving continuity in temperature gradients.
  • Cubic convolution uses 16 neighboring pixels per 30 m pixel, producing sharper gradients than bilinear interpolation (Gao et al., 1996).

Resampling does not increase inherent spatial detail — it fills smaller grid cells with approximated values. Sub-100 m thermal variations remain unresolved, potentially obscuring localized hot spots within heterogeneous urban areas, especially in mixed-use landscapes with varied emissivity.

5. Comparing Landsat LST with Ground-Based Air Temperature

Translating LST into estimates for near-surface air temperature requires specific statistical approaches due to fundamental differences between air and surface temperatures. Empirical relationships can be derived using regression models or machine learning approaches (random forests, support vector machines) in conjunction with land cover data, time of day, and solar angle (Zhou et al., 2019). Adjustments based on time of day — typically early morning or late evening, when surface-air temperature differences are minimized — can improve correlation. Urban areas with low vegetation and high impervious surfaces tend to exhibit higher LST, correlating with elevated air temperatures during daytime peaks but diverging at night due to differing cooling rates.

6. Limitations and Potential Enhancements

Despite resampling, Landsat's 30 m LST does not capture micro-scale temperature variations within a single pixel that ground-based sensors can detect — a real constraint in complex urban areas, where it can substantially underrepresent extreme surface temperatures. Pairing Landsat LST with high-resolution data (drone or aerial thermal imagery, or ground-based point sensors) can address this limitation. Landsat's 16-day revisit interval also limits temporal resolution; multi-satellite fusion with MODIS or VIIRS offers a more complete temperature profile through higher revisit frequency.

Conclusion

Landsat's LST data, derived through radiative transfer models, emissivity correction, and resampling, offers valuable insight into spatial temperature distribution — with inherent limitations when estimating air temperature in heterogeneous urban landscapes. Improvements in resampling and integration with high-resolution datasets allow for more precise temperature approximations and a better understanding of urban heat effects at multiple scales.
